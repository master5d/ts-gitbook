Notebook pack

Extract from sources, not from vibes

A source-grounded notebook is a digest tied to the sources you upload: videos, PDFs, articles, notes. A useful answer does not just sound convincing; it points back to the exact source with a quote.

Gemini Notebook, formerly NotebookLM, is a useful reference for this kind of tool. The free tier is enough for this page: you need a notebook that can read your sources and show where each point came from.

If you do not have a separate notebook tool, the prompts also work in a regular chat with attached files. The discipline is the same: ask for a quote behind every conclusion, otherwise it is an unchecked retelling.

Three source packs

🎬A playlist instead of an evening of watching

A playlist of talks or tutorials has piled up. Watching it all takes an evening; dropping it feels like a waste.

sources: Links to the videos or the whole playlist. In the notebook: "Add source" → "YouTube" — one link per video. The notebook reads subtitles, not the video itself: a video without subtitles stays an empty source.

  1. 1Collect the video links and add each one as a source in a fresh notebook.
  2. 2Ask "Where do these videos disagree with each other?" — disagreements are more useful than a summary.
  3. 3Take "Comparison table" and "Questions with citations" from the prompt kit.
  4. 4Run the verification checklist on two or three answers before trusting the digest.

📚Going deeper on unit materials

A course unit points to external primary sources, and there is no time to read them in full.

sources: Links from the unit "Materials" section: articles, documentation, example catalogs. PDFs can be uploaded as files.

  1. 1Put two or three of the unit’s primary sources in the notebook — not retellings of them.
  2. 2Ask: "What here contradicts the way this is usually retold?"
  3. 3Generate a digest with citations and spot-check a couple of them by clicking into the source.

🗂️Your own documents, questionable

A folder of your own PDFs, notes, or exports that nobody can navigate anymore.

sources: Your own files: PDFs, text, spreadsheets. Personal data stays your call — the notebook only sees what you yourself upload.

  1. 1Pick 5–10 documents on one topic and upload them as sources.
  2. 2Ask the question you opened the folder for — in your own words, no "proper" phrasing.
  3. 3Demand a citation for every conclusion; a conclusion without one is a fabrication candidate.

Prompt kit

Comparison table

Compare how the sources answer [your question] in a table: one row per source, columns — position, argument, quote. The "quote" column must hold a verbatim phrase from the source, not a retelling.

Questions with citations

Answer [your question] using the uploaded sources only. Attach a source quote to every claim. If the sources do not contain the answer, say so directly — do not answer from general knowledge.

Focused audio overview

Create a short audio overview focused on [the topic you find hard]. Have the hosts name which source each point comes from and give a quote — skip any point without a source.

Mind map

Build a mind map linking [topic A] and [topic B] from the sources. For every link, name the source and the quote the link rests on.

Slides from sources

Assemble slides on [topic] strictly from the uploaded sources. Every slide carries a footnote: source and quote. Remove any slide without one.

Verification checklist

  • Click two or three citations: they must land on the exact spot in the source where the phrase actually appears.
  • A confident answer with no citation at all is a retelling. Re-ask and demand citations.
  • Check numbers and percentages against the source separately: that is where retellings drift most.
  • Ask about something you know is not in the sources. The honest reply is "the sources do not cover this," not an answer from memory.
  • Do not pass off a single-source conclusion as consensus — check who else actually claims it.
  • A video without subtitles is an empty source: the notebook "read" its general knowledge, not the video.
